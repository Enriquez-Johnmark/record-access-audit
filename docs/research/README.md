**What this means for the design (recommendation):** the problem is real and well documented, but the public numbers are old or vendor-sourced; there is no good current figure for how often staff snoop. What is solid: the logs are far too large to read, privacy teams are tiny, and OCR settlements keep citing one failure: not regularly reviewing system activity. The two failure modes that matter are opposite: missing the snooper for months (Montefiore, Memorial), and blaming people who did nothing wrong (the Northwestern firings, where "name appeared in a search list" was apparently treated like "opened the chart"). The demo should show both are handled: rules that find planted cases, and evidence shown precisely enough that a reviewer can tell a search-list hit from a chart read.

The demo uses a made-up clinic, staff and patients, so none of these applies to it directly; they shape what the design must show to be credible.

**What this means for the design (recommendation):** the rules put the privacy officer in charge of three judgements the tool must not make: whether an access was a violation, whether it was a breach (the four-factor risk assessment, with its presumption), and what sanction applies. The tool's job is the part HIPAA says must be done "regularly" and which OCR keeps finding was not: examine the log and record that it was examined. So: no automatic "violation" finding, no automatic patient or regulator notice, no automatic message to the staff member or HR. Two timing facts matter. First, the 60-day clock (and California's 15 business days) can start when the clinic "would have known with reasonable diligence"; our reading, not legal advice, is that a flagged case left unreviewed may count, so the tool must show each case's age and never hide or auto-dismiss an open case. Second, sanctions and their reasons must be documented and kept six years, so every decision needs a written reason in the append-only log.

## 3. Detection methods

| Finding | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Explanation-based auditing:** most accesses can be explained by other data (the doctor had an appointment with the patient). Mining frequent explanation templates explained over 94% of accesses in a university health system's log | Fabbri & LeFevre, "Explanation-Based Auditing", PVLDB 5(1), 2011, [dblp](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/pvldb/FabbriL11) (abstract via search) | M |
| Counted more strictly, direct evidence (appointments, visits, medications, documents) explained only about 23% of **first** accesses (a staff member's first look at a patient); adding diagnosis-based responsibility (staff who usually handle that diagnosis) raised it to about 41% at precision 0.904. The 94% applies to all accesses after repeat looks are filtered | Fabbri & LeFevre, JAMIA 2013, [PMC3555324](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3555324) | H |
| **Rule-based flags and their false-positive burden** (Vanderbilt, one week): rules tested were same organisation co-worker, same department co-worker, same last name, home within 0.25 miles, same residential street, and a **first-name** match as a control. In a 100,000-access sample, the first-name control flagged 245, last name 140, proximity 166, street 115, department co-worker 36; organisation co-worker flagged over 3%. Care-relationship evidence explained 16–44% of flagged accesses by rule (department co-worker 43.9%, organisation co-worker 38.8%, last name 21.4%, street 16.1%). The authors: rule-based flagging "can result in high false positives" | Hedda et al., AMIA 2017, [PMC5977720](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5977720) | H |
| **Which features predicted suspicious access** (1,291 labelled events: 643 suspicious, 648 appropriate; reviewers agreed 83–92%): same department (odds ratio 23.5, wide interval), same street address (13.5), same family name (10.4), over 200 accesses in a day (3.7), VIP patient (3.2). Being the patient's provider (0.10) and a care-unit visit match (0.04) pointed the other way. Existing simple rules (VIP, last-name match) "produce many false positives" and cannot rank cases | Boxwala et al., JAMIA 2011, [PMC3128412](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3128412) | H |
| **Machine learning:** logistic regression and SVM reached cross-validated AUC about 0.89–0.91. On 58 truly inappropriate events found by separate investigations, the hospital's rule set caught **0%**; the models caught 76–79% at a 0.5 cut-off. Specificity on that set: not reported (it had no appropriate events) | Boxwala et al. 2011 | H |
| A collaborative-filtering model (per-user and per-patient "fingerprints" from past access) outperformed earlier supervised models on two tertiary hospitals' logs; figures **not read** (full text paywalled) | Menon et al., Machine Learning 95:87–101, 2014, [abstract](https://mlanthology.org/mlj/2014/menon2014mlj-detecting) | M |
| Rules that appear in practice but have **no published evaluation found**: access outside the user's shift, access to a deceased patient's record, access long after discharge, access by a user whose role never touches records (e.g. the security guards in the Yakima case) | Inferred from enforcement cases (section 1) and vendor material; no study **found** | L |
| Self-access (staff opening their own record) is common: about 21,000 of 7.5 million accesses in one week at Vanderbilt | Hedda et al. 2017, Table 1 | H |

**What this means for the design (recommendation):** use two layers, both in code. First, explain away: drop an access when the clinic's own data gives a reason (the user is on the patient's care team, has an encounter or appointment with the patient that day, wrote an order or note). Second, flag what remains when a rule fires: same last name, same address, staff member as patient (co-worker), VIP flag, self-access, high daily volume, no care relationship, outside shift, deceased patient, after discharge, role that should not read charts. Each rule has a reason code, a plain description, a configurable threshold and a version, so the privacy officer can see exactly why a case was raised. Do not train a model to flag; the evidence for ML is real but rests on labelled hospital data we do not have, and the series rule is that code flags. The first-name control result is a warning to put in the README: a name match alone is weak evidence, so name rules should raise priority only together with "no care relationship".

**What this means for the design (recommendation):** model each access as a FHIR R4 `AuditEvent` shaped like a BALP `PatientRead` or `PatientQuery`, served from a pinned local HAPI image as in the earlier projects: `agent.who` → the staff member's `Practitioner` (with `PractitionerRole` for department and role), `entity.what` → `Patient`, `recorded` for the time, `action` R for a read, `subtype` and `entity.query` to tell a search-list hit from opening the chart, `source.site` for the workstation's location. Care relationships come from `Encounter`, `Appointment` and `CareTeam`; shifts, home addresses of staff and VIP flags are clinic data with no clean FHIR home and stay as small saved files. Generate the whole made-up log with our own generator (about 30 staff, 300 patients, 2–4 weeks, normal traffic plus planted cases), because neither Synthea nor any public set gives access logs. Treat HAPI as the read-only source; the tool's own append-only log is the record of review, since HAPI may allow AuditEvents to be changed.

## 5. Safety and fairness design for the model

| Finding | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| A published study of an LLM explaining EHR access alerts to privacy officers: **not found** | searches of PubMed-indexed and arXiv work, 2026-11-11 | n/a |
| Being wrongly accused is a real harm. Northwestern staff said they were fired when a name only appeared on their screen; investigations end up in grievances and arbitration, and Penn Medicine says consistent, evidence-shown interviews helped there | CBS News Chicago, 2019-03-07 (URL left out, see section 1); [Healthcare Innovation 2026](https://www.hcinnovationgroup.com/cybersecurity/privacy-security/article/55409047/how-penn-medicine-investigates-improper-patient-record-access) | M |
| The account is not always the person: in the Memorial and BayCare cases the access came through credentials of a former or outside staff member | section 1 | M |
| Name-match rules fire on coincidence: a first-name control flagged more accesses than the last-name rule, and only 21% of last-name flags were explained by care data | Hedda et al. 2017 | H |
| Grounding by construction: SymGen (MIT 2024) has the model write placeholders that code fills from the source table, so copied values cannot be invented; users verified about 20% faster | [AIhub, 2024-11-15](https://aihub.org/2024/11/15/making-it-easier-to-verify-an-ai-models-responses/) | M |
| LLMs are poor judges of whether a citation is valid (13 models averaged 38% accuracy on bibliographic entries), so checks belong in code, not in a second model | GhostCite, [arXiv 2602.06718](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.06718) | M |
| A vendor's responsible-AI statement says its privacy AI "requires human oversight and feedback to take action", shows the factors behind each prediction, and that decisions stay with the customer — **vendor claim** | [Imprivata Responsible AI Transparency Report, Jan 2025](https://www.imprivata.com/responsible-ai-transparency-report) | M |

**What this means for the design (hypothesis and recommendation):** the model is a writer, not a judge. It receives one flagged case as a list of numbered facts produced by code: pseudonymous staff and patient IDs (S-014, P-203), the staff member's role and department, the access times and actions (search list vs chart opened, how long, which sections by category only), the rule reason codes and their plain descriptions, and the care-relationship facts found and not found ("no encounter, appointment or care-team entry for this pair in the last 90 days"). It never sees names, addresses, dates of birth, diagnoses, notes, results or medications; a section category such as "results" is the most it learns about content. Real names are shown to the privacy officer by the page, not by the model. Its output is parsed into a strict type (summary sentences, each with the fact numbers it relies on; "possible innocent explanations to check"; "questions for the staff member") and checked in code: every sentence cites at least one fact; every number, time and ID in the text appears in the cited facts; no words that decide or accuse ("violated", "snooped", "breach", "guilty", "unauthorized", "inappropriate", "fired", "should be disciplined"); no clinical words; a length limit. A failed check means no explanation: the case shows the facts alone, marked "no explanation". The model never changes which cases are flagged or their order. The page labels the text as an AI summary of the listed facts, not a finding. For due process: show a search-list hit and a chart open differently; show shared-workstation and break-the-glass facts when present; and make "appropriate" a first-class decision, as easy to record as any other.

**What this means for the design (recommendation):** the market is mature, consolidated and AI-heavy, and the demo should not compete on detection. What no vendor publishes: the rule set and its versions in the open, recall and precision on a known labelled set, the checks an AI explanation must pass and their pass rate, the exact facts the model saw, and an append-only log binding each decision to the evidence and the reason. The demo's value is showing those, open and test-backed, in the same pattern as the series: code flags, the model explains, a person decides.

## Open decisions

1. **Scope:** the first version reviews the access log of one made-up clinic (about 30 staff, 300 patients, 2–4 weeks of activity); it flags, explains and records decisions only, with no automatic discipline, no message to the staff member or HR, and no automatic patient or regulator notice. **Recommended answer:** yes.
2. **Explain first, then flag:** code first drops accesses explained by the clinic's own data (care team, encounter or appointment that day, own order or note), then flags what remains when a rule fires; a model never decides which accesses are flagged or their order. **Recommended answer:** yes.
3. **The rule set:** eleven rules, each with a reason code, plain description, configurable threshold and version: same last name, same home address, co-worker as patient, VIP patient, own record, high daily volume, no care relationship, outside shift, deceased patient, long after discharge, and role that should not read charts; a name-match rule raises a case only together with "no care relationship". **Recommended answer:** yes.
4. **Versioned rules:** the rule set is stored as versioned settings the privacy officer edits, every flag records the rule version that raised it, and a change never rewrites past flags. **Recommended answer:** yes.
5. **What the model sees:** only numbered facts built by code, with pseudonymous staff and patient IDs, role, department, access times and actions (search list or chart opened, duration, section categories), rule reasons and the care-relationship facts found and not found; no names, addresses, dates of birth, diagnoses, notes, results or medications. **Recommended answer:** yes.
6. **Checks on every explanation (in code, blocking):** every sentence cites fact numbers; every number, time and ID appears in the cited facts; no deciding or accusing words ("violated", "snooped", "breach", "unauthorized", "inappropriate", "guilty", "fired" and the like); no clinical words; a length limit; a failed check shows the facts with "no explanation" instead. **Recommended answer:** yes.
7. **The privacy officer's decisions:** each case is closed only by the privacy officer as **appropriate**, **needs follow-up with the staff member**, or **possible breach: risk assessment needed**, each with a required written reason; a later decision adds to the history and never replaces it. **Recommended answer:** yes.
8. **Case age and deadlines:** the queue shows each open case's age from the day it was flagged, open cases are never hidden or dismissed automatically, and a "possible breach" case shows a 60-day reminder date from the decision, labelled as a reminder, not legal advice. **Recommended answer:** yes.
9. **Patient access report:** include a page where a made-up patient sees which clinic roles and departments looked at their record and when (no staff names), as a demonstration of the withdrawn HITECH access report, clearly marked as beyond what HIPAA now requires. **Recommended answer:** yes.
10. **Data format:** accesses are FHIR R4 `AuditEvent`s shaped like IHE BALP `PatientRead` and `PatientQuery`, with `Practitioner`, `PractitionerRole`, `Patient`, `Encounter`, `Appointment` and `CareTeam`, all made up by our own generator and served from a pinned local HAPI server; HAPI is read-only to the tool, and the tool's append-only log is the record of review. **Recommended answer:** yes.
11. **Measurement:** a labelled made-up log with planted cases of each kind (co-worker, relative at the same address, VIP, own record, no care relationship, shared login, plus look-alike innocent cases); report rule recall and precision per rule, the share of accesses explained away, and the explanation check pass rate; no claim of catching real snooping. **Recommended answer:** yes.
12. **Model and claims:** Claude Sonnet through the owner's API key (`OWNER_ANTHROPIC_API_KEY`); the README states that real use needs a provider that signs a BAA, says what the code enforces and how it is tested, and never claims HIPAA compliance or certification. **Recommended answer:** yes.
